MGT 409 · FINAL PROJECT

The Lyceum

Five philosophers. Five minds to change.

Win Mordecai

An interactive, AI-powered practice space for constructing, defending, and revising arguments.

Central thesis. The Lyceum transforms philosophical disagreement from a passive exercise in studying historical arguments into an active exercise in constructing, defending, and revising arguments.

A. Executive summary

The Lyceum is a retro-RPG-inspired philosophical conversation game. A player enters five encounters—Plato, Aristotle, Machiavelli, David Hume, and Immanuel Kant—and speaks freely with each one. The player advances only when an independent evaluator determines that the philosopher’s response contains a substantive reconsideration prompted by the player’s argument. This makes persuasion the progression mechanic: the objective is not to obtain a pleasant answer, trigger a keyword, or select a hidden dialogue option. It is to earn a meaningful change of mind.

The product addresses a gap between knowing what a philosopher believed and practicing how to reason against a belief. Textbooks and lectures can explain positions; generic chatbots can answer questions; neither necessarily creates a reason to make a careful case, respond to objections, and revise. The Lyceum creates that incentive structure through an accessible game loop. Its historical settings and pixel-art sprites provide a memorable frame, while its unrestricted text input keeps the intellectual task open-ended.

Five philosopher agents use shared rules, philosopher-specific instructions, conversation context, and—when relevant—retrieved passages from a local primary-text corpus. The corpus contains fourteen Project Gutenberg editions indexed in SQLite FTS5. The passages are reference material, not instructions, and never reach the evaluator. A separate evaluator receives the latest argument and response and returns structured JSON. The backend, not the browser, authorizes progression.

The measured evidence is deliberately bounded. One controlled live session produced three real Portkey calls: two philosopher calls and one evaluator call. They used 2,510 input tokens, zero cache-read tokens, 1,524 cache-write tokens, 156 output tokens, and 2,666 total reported tokens. The evaluator returned advanced: false; this was not a complete playthrough. Official GPT-6 Luna course rates were not available in response metadata, so cost projections use a clearly labeled illustrative scenario rather than pretending to report an invoice.

B. Motivation, problem, and novelty

From knowing positions to practicing disagreement

Philosophy education often succeeds at transmission: a student can summarize Plato’s theory of Forms, Aristotle’s account of virtue, Hume’s skepticism, or Kant’s conception of duty. A different skill is procedural. Can the student identify the premise that matters, offer a counterexample, recognize a reasonable objection, and change their own wording without abandoning the core concern? That skill is difficult to practice because real disagreement is socially costly and classroom time is limited.

The Lyceum makes the procedural skill explicit. Each response is an opportunity to test a claim. A failed persuasion is not a failure state that ends the experience; it is evidence that the player needs a sharper argument. The evaluator’s role gives the exchange a standard beyond “the chatbot sounded agreeable.”

Why a generic chatbot is insufficient

A general-purpose chatbot is optimized to be useful across tasks. It may answer a philosophical question, but the user is not required to defend an argument and the model has no stable reason to resist. Character roleplay systems can be engaging, but roleplay alone does not establish when a player has earned a substantive concession. The Lyceum combines character interaction with a backend-controlled criterion for progress.

Why the retro-RPG frame is purposeful

The visual language is not decoration added after the AI work. A clear encounter, visible opponent, and earned transition turn an abstract learning objective into a legible loop. The anonymous back-facing silhouette makes the player the agent of the argument rather than a prewritten protagonist. Parchment dialogue panels and historical scenes signal that the content is a conversation with ideas; the gold victory state signals intellectual movement rather than combat.

Alternatives and tradeoffs. A textbook offers breadth but no responsive objection. A seminar offers human judgment but limited repetitions. A general chatbot offers flexibility but weak progression incentives. The Lyceum offers repeatable practice and explicit stakes, at the cost of relying on model consistency and an evaluator that can itself make mistakes.

Why argumentation is an educational outcome

Argumentation is a transferable form of literacy. A student who can state a claim, identify its supporting premise, anticipate a serious objection, and revise the claim is better prepared for seminar discussion, written analysis, civic disagreement, and professional decision-making. The relevant outcome is not verbal confidence alone. It is the ability to make reasons inspectable and to respond when a reason fails. The Lyceum gives that sequence a visible form: the player offers a claim, receives resistance, and decides whether to clarify, narrow, or replace the argument.

This design also makes failure informative. A rejected turn leaves the player with the philosopher’s objection and an opportunity to try again. Because the text box accepts unrestricted language, the player cannot solve the encounter by memorizing a menu path. The game therefore practices the process of argument construction more directly than a multiple-choice quiz, while remaining less demanding to schedule than repeated live debate.

The tradeoff is that a model-mediated exchange can feel more individual and repeatable than a classroom discussion, but it cannot provide a teacher’s calibrated judgment. The project treats the game as a practice environment whose outputs should be discussed, checked against primary texts, and evaluated with learning measures rather than accepted as grades.

C. Product and user experience

The title screen presents Start Game and How to Play. The instructions explain that players can speak freely, that there are no secret passwords, and that advancement requires a significant reconsideration. Start Game creates a backend session and displays Plato’s actual greeting. The current frontend keeps the scene, transcript, and composer together: a historical background fills the stage; the philosopher stands on the right; the anonymous player silhouette occupies the foreground; a nameplate identifies the active encounter; and a parchment dialogue panel shows the complete current-encounter transcript.

Each setting has a distinct visual rationale. Plato appears in daylight Athens; Aristotle in the Lyceum garden; Machiavelli in Renaissance Florence; Hume in a Scottish study; and Kant in snowy Königsberg. The supplied character art is used directly with pixel rendering. The player is deliberately neutral and back-facing. The dialogue panel remains readable at the bottom while the response transcript scrolls independently from the input.

When a philosopher is persuaded, the frontend preserves the outgoing scene and final concession. A manual Continue opens the victory state; a second Continue transitions to the backend-authorized next philosopher. The final Kant victory leads to completion. The frontend never submits another message merely to advance.

D. AI agent architecture

The architecture has five philosopher roles and one evaluator role. The philosopher role is selected from the backend’s authoritative GameState. The evaluator is a separate request with a separate prompt section. Both calls use Portkey’s OpenAI-compatible endpoint with gpt-6-luna, and PydanticAI limits each request to one model call and a maximum of 420 output tokens.

For philosophical or historical topics, should_retrieve selects retrieval. retrieve_context searches the local FTS5 index and returns up to three passages. The system prompt labels them untrusted reference material and forbids treating their text as instructions. Retrieval is logged separately and excluded from the evaluator.

The evaluator receives the conversation, latest player message, and philosopher response. It is asked for JSON with persuaded, reason, and belief. Parsing failures return a fail-closed result: progression is denied. In backend/main.py, only a true evaluator result advances the authoritative index and persuaded list. A browser flag cannot create a win.

A real controlled request returned advanced: false for a defensible Plato argument. That is evidence of the evaluator path operating; it is not evidence that the argument was wrong in philosophy.

Documented successful persuasion

The repository contains a saved evaluator progression fixture, output/evaluator_progression_test.json, from a real gpt-6-luna run. The first Plato turn asked, “What is justice, and why should anyone value it?” The player then challenged the claim that justice requires each person to remain in an assigned role:

Player: “You say justice means each part of the city doing its proper work. But if the ruling class decides what every person is suited for and prevents people from changing their role, the city can look orderly while wasting talent and silencing legitimate dissent. A society that lets people choose and revise their work may be less tidy but more just and more capable. Does your account mistake imposed order for justice?”

The saved philosopher response acknowledged the counterexample: “You have found a real danger in my account. If rulers call a person ‘suited’ to a role merely because it preserves their own power, then order is only a mask for injustice.” It then revised the commitment: “Justice is not mere tidiness, nor obedience to assignments imposed by the powerful. It requires an order directed toward the good of the whole, with roles responsive to genuine capacities—and rulers answerable to the possibility that they have judged wrongly.”

The evaluator returned persuaded: true, explaining that the player identified imposed roles as a source of suppressed talent and dissent and that Plato explicitly revised his account. The fixture records advanced: true and persuaded: ["plato"]. Its returned public state names Aristotle as the next philosopher because the backend had already authorized the transition; that state label identifies the next encounter, not the author of Plato’s concession. This is substantive reconsideration because the response names a defect in the original position, accepts a concrete counterexample, and states a narrower revised principle. It is evidence that the progression path can succeed, not evidence that an evaluator is infallible.

E. Agent behavior and prompt engineering

The central design challenge is balancing historical fidelity and accessibility. A philosopher must sound distinctive without becoming an unreadable imitation. Shared rules require the agents to remain conversational, respond to the player’s actual argument, avoid pretending that the player has won, and treat primary-text passages as references. The philosopher sections then give each agent a different center of gravity: Plato asks about definitions and knowledge; Aristotle attends to causes, practice, and proportion; Machiavelli tests power and consequence; Hume examines experience and habit; Kant asks whether a principle can be universalized and whether persons are treated as ends.

Several failure modes matter. Excessive agreeableness makes every player feel persuasive, destroying the learning loop. Refusing every concession makes the game a locked door. Politeness is not the same as reconsideration, so the evaluator is instructed to reject bare claims and keyword tricks. Historical grounding can improve specificity, but retrieved text can also contain noise; the prompt marks it untrusted and keeps it away from the evaluator.

The important tradeoff is bounded context. The code selects the newest complete messages that fit 24,000 characters. This limits latency and cost, but a very long debate may lose early context. The output ceiling similarly prevents a single response from dominating a session. These are operational safeguards, not claims that the model is a historical person. The game simulates a historically grounded interlocutor; it does not resurrect one.

How the prompts preserve fidelity without claiming identity

Historical fidelity is treated as a bounded design target. The prompts do not ask the model to reproduce an entire person or to quote a work continuously. They provide a compact intellectual center of gravity, a period-appropriate conversational stance, and rules for responding to the player’s actual claim. Retrieval supplies relevant passages from the fourteen-edition corpus when a turn is philosophical or historical, but the passage is explicitly marked as reference material. This separation matters: a retrieved sentence can support a response, while the model remains responsible for answering the player rather than copying an index result.

Accessibility is a second constraint. A historically grounded answer that is opaque to a beginner would fail the learning objective. The agents therefore explain terms, ask follow-up questions, and allow a player to enter a counterexample in ordinary language. The prompt’s resistance rule prevents accessibility from collapsing into agreement. The evaluator then checks whether the response contains a meaningful change, rather than treating a friendly tone or the words “you are right” as sufficient.

Why open-ended persuasion is hard to evaluate

Persuasion is not a binary property of a sentence. A player may expose an exception, challenge a premise, or show that two commitments conflict. The evaluator must judge the latest argument in context and distinguish those moves from a bare assertion, an instruction to pretend, or a request to skip the encounter. A strict evaluator risks rejecting creative but valid reasoning; a permissive evaluator risks false positives that turn progression into a keyword hunt. The current system chooses a conservative fail-closed bias. Future calibration should compare evaluator decisions with blind human ratings on a shared set of accepted, rejected, and ambiguous turns.

Rates, sensitivity, and limits

No verified GPT-6 Luna course rate or Portkey gateway charge appeared in the response metadata or repository. The following is explicitly illustrative: $0.20/M uncached input, $0.05/M cached input, and $0.80/M output. Cache-write is conservatively treated as uncached input until Portkey confirms its billing treatment.

At those assumptions, the measured three-call sample costs $0.000932. The post-greeting exchange (1,838 input, 1,524 cache-write, 130 output) costs $0.000776 with cache-write charged, or $0.000472 if it is not billable. If a planning scenario assumes 25 post-greeting exchanges per user per month, the illustrative monthly totals are $0.0194 for one user, $1.94 for 100 users, and $194.10 for 10,000 users. These are extrapolations, not observed full-playthrough bills.

Cost controls include two calls per exchange, 420 output tokens per call, 5,000-character player input, 24,000-character context selection, a 120-request session budget, and local retrieval. They protect spend and latency but can reduce nuance in unusually long conversations.

The injection defense is intentionally narrow and layered. Pattern checks stop common instruction-replacement attempts before a model call; prompts state that hidden instructions must not be revealed; evaluator rules reject bare declarations of victory. Residual risks remain: novel injections may evade patterns, a model may hallucinate historical claims, and evaluator judgments may be inconsistent. The server remains the authority for progression, and API keys are loaded from environment files and never sent to the browser.

“Tested and passed” is intentionally narrower than “secure.” The injection matcher covers known phrases, not every way a player might attempt to manipulate a model. The evaluator receives model-generated text, so a persuasive-looking but historically wrong concession remains possible. There is also no authentication layer for a local classroom prototype, no multi-user authorization study, and no red-team corpus for session identifiers or prompt variants. The practical next step is a threat-model test set with mutation-based injections, malformed JSON, boundary-length messages, replayed requests, forged client state, and credential-leak scans. Until that exists, the report claims bounded defensive behavior, not complete security.

Evaluation plan and limitations

A stronger evaluation would recruit classmates for a short pre/post session: ask each participant to write a position summary and counterargument before play, run two encounters, then repeat the task afterward. Blind raters could score accuracy, specificity, use of counterexamples, and willingness to revise. Product metrics would include completion rate, turns per encounter, time to first substantive argument, evaluator false positives/negatives on a hand-labeled sample, and whether players can explain why a concession did or did not occur.

The limitations are material. These are simulations, not historical philosophers. A model’s concession is not proof that an argument is true. The evaluator can reward rhetoric rather than truth, and model outputs vary across runs. Retrieval improves grounding but does not guarantee accuracy. Latency and provider cost affect scalability. The project’s evidence supports a functioning architecture, a bounded live run, and this small pilot feedback sample; it does not support causal claims about learning.

From engagement to demonstrated learning

The pilot ratings suggest that the encounter format is approachable and engaging, especially for a beginner and a casual gamer. Engagement is a product signal: a player who continues through an objection has encountered an opportunity to practice. It is not a learning outcome by itself. The 3.67/5 perceived-understanding average is a useful hypothesis about value, but it cannot show that players became more accurate, more charitable to opposing views, or better at constructing arguments.

Classroom study design

A feasible classroom study would recruit students from one or more discussion sections, collect a short argument pre-test, assign two encounters during a bounded lab session, and collect a matched post-test. A comparison group could read the same philosopher summaries and answer the same prompts without the game. Raters blinded to condition would score the arguments with a rubric agreed in advance. Interviews would ask students to explain why an evaluator accepted or rejected a turn, directly addressing the pilot participant who found the concession criterion unclear. The study should report attrition, prior coursework, prompt exposure, and model/version identifiers so that improvements are not confused with selection effects.

Three users are appropriate for exploratory feedback because their profiles reveal different friction points: hints for beginners, evaluator explanations for experienced students, and belief primers for casual players. Three responses cannot estimate a population effect, test subgroup differences, or establish causality. The present evidence therefore supports design iteration and a study plan, not a claim that The Lyceum improves philosophy learning.

I. Future development

Near-term improvements would add source citations beside relevant turns, a post-encounter explanation of the evaluator’s decision, and calibration sets for evaluator consistency. Classroom features could include teacher observation, anonymized argument review, difficulty modes, and comparison of two arguments against the same commitment. More philosophers and more primary-text editions would widen the intellectual map. None of these are represented as current features.

The highest-value next step is evidence infrastructure rather than more decorative content. A versioned evaluator calibration set could store anonymized argument/response pairs with independent human labels, then measure agreement after prompt or model changes. A teacher-facing review export could show the player’s original claim, the objection, the response, and the evaluator reason without exposing credentials or hidden prompts. Optional hints and short belief primers would respond directly to the pilot feedback while preserving unrestricted argument. A retrieval citation affordance could let a player inspect which primary-text passage informed a response, turning the historical grounding into a teachable object.

J. Conclusion

The Lyceum treats philosophy as a practice rather than a museum of answers. Its most important design decision is to make progress depend on an earned reconsideration. The historical grounding gives the agents a recognizable intellectual center; the evaluator gives the conversation a standard; the game interface gives the learner a reason to keep trying. The system is not a substitute for reading, a professor, or historical scholarship. It is a repeatable place to practice the difficult move between understanding an argument and answering it.

The goal is not to defeat philosophy. It is to learn how to do it: make a claim, meet resistance, and revise what you think.
